\subsection*{Response 1B -- Why the rule must depend on context}

\begin{figure}[h]\centering
\includegraphics[width=.85\linewidth]{1.2-1.pdf}
\caption{Output 1.2: Station 1, validation; Product A (period 16.1) and Product C (period 30.1).}
\end{figure}

The ridge map $\widehat W$ in Eq.~(8) is fitted once, over windows whose periods range from 14 to
36 samples. For a given context, where the next peak falls depends on the period. One fixed linear
operator cannot pick the right phase advance for each context, so least squares ends up returning
something like an average of the continuations for all paces. Averaging sine waves of different
periods gives a forecast with a smaller amplitude and a blurred phase.

Output 1.2 shows exactly this. For Product A, shared and sensor-specific ridge get the first peak
roughly right and then flatten out: the second and third peaks are about 1\,m/s$^2$ too low and
the troughs about 1\,m/s$^2$ too high (window RMSE 0.78, against 0.23 for period-routed ridge). For
Product C they miss the second trough and peak around 1.5--2\,s (0.45 vs.\ 0.15). The error grows
with lead time because the phase mismatch builds up.

Attention avoids this compromise because its mixing weights are recomputed for every input. The
projections $W_Q, W_K, W_V$ are fixed after training, but the weight matrix
$\mathrm{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt{d_h})$ is a function of the current window. In Output 1.3 the same
Station 1 projections give clearly different maps for the Product A and Product C contexts. The
differences are concentrated on the most recent keys, with a maximum absolute difference of 0.43
and a mean of 0.013. So the way values are mixed changes with the input, whereas ridge applies the
same $\widehat W$ to both contexts. Output 1.3 only shows that the weights depend on the context;
it does not show that Attention has found the physical period.

\subsection*{Response 2 -- Separating slow structure from repetition}

I selected a width of $m=41$.

\textbf{What changes.} In Output 2.1 (Station 2, Product C, validation), the narrow widths (17 and
25) leave a trend that still wiggles with the vibration. Part of the cycle leaks into $T$, and the
remainder is damped (residual std 0.58\,m/s$^2$ at $m=17$). As $m$ grows, the trend becomes a
smooth ramp and the remainder becomes a full-size oscillation centred on zero (std 1.57 at
$m=41$). After decomposition the projection score has a sharp peak at the true period (32); on the
raw context it is almost flat. Near the right edge, replicate padding makes the last $\approx$20
samples of $T$ flatten towards the final value, which is visible in the plot.

In the oracle diagnostic (Output 2.2, established stations, training data only), the share of
windows whose period is recovered to within one sample rises from 68.7\% on the raw context to
98.0, 98.9, 99.3 and 99.8\% for $m=$ 17, 25, 33 and 41. In the ablation (Output 2.3, validation),
adding decomposition to the same Attention forecaster lowers RMSE for every product in both
populations:

Even so, it is still well behind period-routed ridge (0.15--0.18).

\textbf{Why a moving average suits this data.} The signal is a slow component (240-sample time
scale) plus a fast cycle (14--36 samples). A moving average is a low-pass filter. When its width is
at least one vibration period, the cycle roughly averages out inside the window, while the slow
part, which is nearly linear over 41 samples, passes through. The trend head can then extend the
ramp linearly, and the encoder sees a zero-mean repeating signal.

\textbf{Why the oracle diagnostic is a fair way to choose $m$.} The point of the split is to expose
the repetition, and period recovery measures that directly. It uses the generator's true period,
but only to score training windows from the established stations, so validation, Station 4 and
the test set play no part in choosing $m$. The true period is never a model input.

\textbf{Where it would fail.} A centred moving average assumes the trend is locally linear and much
slower than the window. An abrupt level shift, such as a calibration jump, would be smeared over
$m$ samples and leave a step-shaped artefact in $S$. An alternative is STL/LOESS or a Fourier
low-pass split, which assumes the trend is simply the low-frequency band. It copes better with
curvature, but it needs a cut-off frequency and is more expensive to apply inside every block.

\subsection*{Response 3 -- Mixing by temporal delay}

I implemented \texttt{aggregate\_delays} (Eq.~6) with a per-example \texttt{gather} on the index
$(t-\tau_j)\bmod L$. On the worked example it returns $z=[35,15,25,25]$; $z_0=35$ confirms that the
shift runs in the right direction. The notebook's value and gradient checks pass, and Output 3.1
confirms the supplied \texttt{delay\_scores} on $[1,3,1,3]$ (scores $[4,-4,4,-4]$).

\begin{figure}[h]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{3.2-1.pdf}
\caption{Output 3.2: signal-level recurrence diagnostic (validation; Station 2, Product A,
$P\approx16.1$). This is a correlation computed directly from the signal, \emph{not} the
model's learned $q/k$ scores.}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Strongest lags.} The residual correlation peaks at delays 16 (0.896) and 32 (0.897), the
integers closest to $P=16.1$ and $2P=32.3$. It drops to about $-0.9$ near $P/2\approx8$,
$3P/2\approx24$ and $5P/2\approx40$, and rises again towards $3P\approx48$. So the recurrence sits
on whole multiples of the run's period.

\textbf{Why removing the drift helps.} On the raw signal the correlation just decays from 0.45 to
about $-0.55$ across the band, with no peak at $P$. A rising ramp looks similar to itself at every
small shift, and the circular wrap joins the high end of the ramp to its low start, so the ramp
dominates the score. Once the trend is removed, what is left oscillates around zero: aligned peaks
add up and misaligned ones cancel. Top-$K$ selection then picks $P$ and its multiples rather than
the smallest delays. This supports the delay mixer's assumption that, once the drift is gone, the
useful relationships are organised by a few displacements.

\textbf{When this assumption is wrong.} Take an isolated transient, such as a single impact inside
the context. Delay aggregation copies values from $t-\tau_j$, so the spike gets replayed every
$\tau_j$ samples into the representation and the forecast, creating echoes of an event that will
not happen again. A period that drifts within the window, as when a machine is spinning up, is
another problem: no single lag lines up the whole window, the correlation peaks smear out, and the
blended shifted copies drift out of phase. Pairwise Attention judges each position on its own
content, so it does not suffer from either problem in the same way.

\subsection*{2.1 What the data look like}

\begin{figure}[h]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{t2_eda.pdf}
\caption{Training target: last 1000 steps, ACF, and periodogram.}
\end{figure}

The series is episodic and heavy-tailed: the median is 73, the 99th percentile 418 and the
maximum 994. It builds up over episodes lasting a few days and then drops sharply. The periodogram
is ``red'' (power rises towards long periods), so most of the variance comes from these irregular
episodes rather than from a regular cycle.

Its memory is short. The ACF is 0.97 at lag 1, 0.57 at 12, 0.40 at 24, 0.16 at 48 and about 0.02
by 96. Beyond about two days, the past of the target says little about its future, so most of the
168-step horizon lies outside what the target's own history can predict.

There are no timestamps, but the periodogram has clear peaks at 24 and its harmonic 12, and the
ACF has small bumps at 24, 48 and 72. I therefore treat one step as one hour with a daily cycle,
and add the phase $(\texttt{time\_idx} \bmod 24)$ as $\sin/\cos$ features. This is the only thing
I derive from the index. The ACF at 168 is only 0.03, so I do not model a weekly cycle.

The external file has six continuous features (A--F) and four binary ones (G--J), exactly one of
which is active at each step. They look like weather measurements, with G--J something like a
wind-direction category. Feature D (correlation $-0.35$ with $\log(1{+}y)$) and indicator H
($-0.35$; the target averages 70 when H is active against 125 for J) are the most informative. This
suggests the covariates drive when episodes start and end.

\subsection*{2.2 Validation protocol}

I split the data chronologically. Training targets end before step 39{,}120, and validation
targets are the last 4{,}536 observed steps (about 6 months), immediately before the hidden block
that starts after step 43{,}656. Validation forecasts start every 24 steps, which gives 183
overlapping 168-step forecasts. I report pooled RMSE, MAE and sMAPE, plus the mean RMSE per block.
A single leaderboard score is just one draw from this spread of block scores, so I treat it as a
check rather than something to tune on.

All scalers are fitted on the training region only (mean and standard deviation, with $\log1p$
applied first to the skewed cumulative features D, E and F). Every configuration is run with seeds
0, 1 and 2 and reported as mean $\pm$ std; I only treat a difference as real if it is clearly
larger than the spread across seeds.

On the same 183 blocks, the simple baselines score (RMSE): training mean 87.8, mean of the last 7
days 94.8, seasonal naive (lag 24) 117.9 and persistence 122.2. The training mean beats persistence
because 168 steps is far beyond the ACF memory, which shows how hard this horizon is.

%---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection*{2.3 Model}

I wrote the Autoformer from scratch, following Wu et al.\ (2021, \S\S3.1--3.2) and the structure
of the authors' reference code (\url{https://github.com/thuml/Autoformer}). It contains both
mechanisms the assignment requires.

\textbf{Series decomposition.} A centred moving average (kernel 25, about one day, with replicate
padding) is applied to the input and inside every encoder layer (twice) and decoder layer (three
times). The decoder adds up the trend parts it removes, through a projection, into a trend stream
that starts from the input's trend and mean.

\textbf{Auto-Correlation} replaces dot-product attention. Delay scores
$R(\tau)=\sum_t q_t k_{t-\tau}$ are computed with the FFT, the top $k=\lfloor \ln L\rfloor$ delays
are kept, their scores are turned into softmax weights, and values $v_{t-\tau}$ are aggregated per
example. This is the same convention as my Task 1 \texttt{aggregate\_delays}. The reference code
shares delays across the batch during training and reads $v_{t+\tau}$; I kept the Task 1
convention so that the value read back comes from the key position that produced the score.

\textbf{Inputs.} The encoder sees the last $L$ target values plus, at every step, the covariates
(external features and daily phase) through a linear embedding. The decoder gets the seasonal part
of the last 48 steps, zeros for the 168 future steps, and the covariates over both. This is how
covariates that are known for the future enter the model.

\textbf{Size and training.} I used $d_\text{model}=32$, 4 heads, $d_\text{ff}=64$, and one encoder
and one decoder layer, about 22k trainable parameters. Since the leaderboard penalises parameters,
I started small and planned to grow the model only if validation justified it. The loss is MSE on
the standardised target, which matches the RMSE ranking. I used AdamW (learning rate $10^{-3}$,
weight decay $10^{-4}$), batch size 64, gradient clipping at 1, and every 4th training window per
epoch, with early stopping on validation RMSE (patience 2).

The external file helps a lot, but only the part covering the forecast window. Adding the future
covariates lowers RMSE by 18.5 (about 21\%), more than ten times the seed standard deviation. Past
covariates alone change RMSE by only 0.7, which is within seed noise.

I think the reason is the short memory of the series. The target forgets its past within 2--4
days, and past covariates mostly repeat what the past target already shows. What decides the next
week is when episodes start and end, and that depends on conditions during the week itself, such
as a change in the G--J category or a jump in D. That is why these covariates need to go into the
decoder, aligned with the forecast steps; putting them only in the encoder wastes them.

Without the future covariates, the Autoformer does not even beat the training mean (89.6 vs.\ 87.8).
For a 168-step horizon on a short-memory series, the target's own history adds nothing over its
mean.

The metrics also disagree: with future covariates, sMAPE improves by 11\% but RMSE by 21\%. The
gain is concentrated on large values (episode peaks and drops), which dominate squared error, while
sMAPE is driven by low-level stretches where relative errors stay large.

\subsection*{2.6 Final submission and what the leaderboard taught me}

\textbf{Attempt \#1: a single refit model.} I submitted the $\log1p$ model with seed 0. Early
stopping on validation ran 7 epochs (best epoch 5, validation RMSE 67.46), and I then refit it on
the full history for 5 epochs ($P=22{,}081$, $E=7+5=12$). It scored RMSE 142.04 (MAE 96.4, sMAPE
99.3\%, score 142.04). That is around the 97th percentile of my validation block RMSEs (median 49),
and the sMAPE close to 100\% means the forecast was far too low. The model had predicted a ``clean''
week (median 23.6), which the horizon covariates seemed to support.

\textbf{Diagnosis.} One bad score can be bad luck, so before using another attempt I checked
whether the method itself was fragile, using training and validation data only
(\texttt{diagnose*.py}). I found three problems:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The forecast for a single week is unstable. Across seeds 0--2, with and without refit, the
  mean of the hidden-week forecast ranged from 26 to 71, and the six forecasts differed from each
  other by an RMSE of 28.6 on average. The model I submitted gave the lowest of the six.
  \item I never validated the refit step. For seed 0 it moved the forecast mean from 71 (the
  validated model) to 26. Pooled validation RMSE averages this kind of variation away; a single
  168-step block does not.
  \item The log target is biased low in high-level weeks: the mean bias is $-33.7$ on the top
  quarter of validation blocks.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Attempt \#2: averaging three validated models, with no refit.} I chose this on validation:

I should be clear about how much of this is luck. On validation the gain was only about 1.9 RMSE,
and the confidence interval for the very similar equal mix included zero, so part of the 10-point
leaderboard gain is luck on one week. The raw models also forecast a higher week, which attempt
\#1 had already hinted was the truth. I chose the candidate and its weight on validation only, but
it was attempt \#1's result that made me spend another attempt.

\textbf{What I learned.} The size penalties barely matter here: score minus RMSE was 0.006, 0.009
and 0.024 for attempts 1--3, with $P$ ranging from 22k to 221k. Designs differ by tens of RMSE
points, so reducing variance is worth far more than saving parameters. A good pooled validation
score also does not guarantee a good score on one block. I should have checked how stable the
forecast was at the real forecast origin before my first submission, and I should not have
submitted a refit model I had never validated. Finally, the leaderboard results prompted my
diagnosis and my decision to use more attempts, but every submitted model and setting was chosen
on the validation evidence above. I left two of the five attempts unused.

\subsection*{2.7 Limitations}
My validation period (the last 6 months) is one stretch of the series; if the hidden week behaves
differently, the validation ranking may not carry over. The model also takes the future
covariates as exact, ignoring any measurement noise in them. Finally, the daily-phase features
assume the 24-step cycle seen in training continues into the forecast window.

\subsection*{2.8 Reflection questions}

\textbf{Q2.1 -- Auto-Correlation vs.\ self-attention.} Auto-Correlation is the better bias for
stationary signals built from a few stable periods. There every pair $(t, t-P)$ is related for the
same reason, and a single score $R(P)$ pools that evidence over the whole window at $O(L\log L)$
cost, whereas attention has to rediscover the relation pair by pair at $O(L^2)$. Task 1 is an
example: delay mixing alone cut MSE by 70\%, against 47\% for decomposition. What it throws away is
anything that is not a shift of the whole series, such as a link to one specific position (``the
value right after the last regime change'') or a single precursor event, because it copies whole
shifted sequences. My Task 2 series, with its red, episodic spectrum, has little periodic structure
for it to use, which is one reason the model without covariates could not beat the mean. If the
period drifts within a window, no single lag lines everything up: the peaks of $R(\tau)$ widen and
shrink, and the shifted copies cancel each other out. Attention degrades more gracefully because
each query picks its own keys, but it still has to learn the drift.

\textbf{Q2.2 -- What the moving-average trend assumes.} It assumes the trend is roughly linear over
the kernel and much slower than the seasonal part. At the window edges, replicate padding pulls the
last $k/2$ trend values towards the final value, so the trend flattens exactly at the forecast
origin, where it matters most (Output 2.1 in Task 1 shows this). With curvature, the average
undershoots at the peaks and the curvature leaks into the seasonal part. An abrupt level shift
gets smeared into a ramp of width $k$, leaving an S-shaped artefact in the seasonal component. An
alternative is STL (LOESS-based) decomposition, which assumes only that the trend is smooth, not
linear, and is robust to outliers. In return it needs more hyperparameters, costs more, and is not
a simple differentiable layer to put in every block. A median filter would keep level shifts sharp
but is not smooth.

\textbf{Q2.3 -- Progressive vs.\ one-shot decomposition.} Inside the network, the mixing and
feed-forward layers keep creating new slow components in the learned representation, for example
through residual connections. Decomposing again in every block removes them before the next mixer,
so each Auto-Correlation sees a roughly zero-mean seasonal stream. One-shot preprocessing only
touches the raw input and cannot do this. Interleaving also lets the decoder build up the trend
layer by layer. It can hurt when the useful seasonal signal is slower than the kernel: with a
weekly cycle and $k=25$ on hourly data, every block pushes more of that cycle into the trend path,
which is only a linear projection and cannot model it. Applying the same filter repeatedly also
compounds the edge bias.

\textbf{Q2.4 -- $k = c\log L$.} A larger $k$ blends more lags, which hedges when the right lag is
uncertain but also mixes in weak or spurious lags and blurs sharp periodic structure. A smaller
$k$ commits to the strongest lag: precise when it is right, costly when it is wrong, and unable to
represent several periods at once. The $\log L$ form is mainly convenient, since it keeps the total
cost at $O(L\log L)$ like the FFT and grows slowly as longer windows add harmonics of the same
periods; I do not know of a statistical argument for it. Ideally $k$ would follow the number of
distinct periods and the multiples worth keeping (for Task 2, 24, 12 and their multiples), chosen
from how significant the peaks of $R(\tau)$ are rather than from $L$.

\textbf{Q2.5 -- One-shot vs.\ autoregressive horizons.} An autoregressive model is trained on true
inputs but fed its own predictions at test time, so errors compound and the inputs drift away from
anything seen in training (exposure bias). A one-shot model, like Autoformer, avoids both. The
error structure also differs: autoregressive errors are strongly correlated along the horizon,
because one early miss shifts everything after it, while direct-forecast errors are less coupled
but the forecast can be less consistent, for example smoothed towards the mean, as my model does
beyond the ACF memory. Autoregressive forecasting suits low-noise, nearly deterministic dynamics and
short horizons; direct forecasting suits noisy, heavy-tailed series and long horizons, and its
advantage grows as the horizon gets longer.

\textbf{Q2.6 -- Window-relative normalisation.} It throws away the absolute level and scale of the
window, i.e.\ where the series sits within its long-run range. That matters when the future depends
on the level: mean reversion, physical limits, or regimes defined by level (an episode at 400
behaves differently from a calm spell at 20). It helps a lot for a series whose level drifts but
whose shape is stable, such as sales growing over years with the same weekly pattern. It provably
loses something for a mean-reverting series: two windows with the same shape at $+3\sigma$ and
$-3\sigma$ need opposite forecasts, but they look identical after normalisation, unless the
removed statistics are fed back in. My Task 2 series behaves like this, since at $H=168$ the
global mean (87.8) beats the window mean (94.8), which is why I used global scaling fitted on the
training data.

\textbf{Q2.7 -- Past-known vs.\ future-known covariates.} The distinction changes where they should
enter, not just how much they help. Covariates known only up to the origin belong in the encoder
with the target history. Covariates known over the horizon must also reach the decoder, aligned
with the forecast steps, so that step $t+h$ can use $x_{t+h}$; putting them only in the encoder
wastes exactly the useful part. A fast-acting driver with little persistence, such as wind or rain
during the hour being forecast, is informative only when its future is known, and my ablation
shows this (past only 88.9, close to none at 89.6; past and future 71.1). A slow or deterministic
covariate, such as the daily phase or a holiday calendar, is equally useful in both cases, because
its past already determines its future.

\textbf{Q2.8 -- No calendar features.} Without timestamps the model loses hour-of-day, day-of-week
and seasonal effects, holidays and other irregular calendar events, and it cannot align cycles at
different scales without knowing the base unit. Periodic structure can still be recovered from
peaks in the periodogram and ACF (here 24 and 12, i.e.\ a daily cycle in hourly data) and then
encoded as $\sin/\cos(2\pi\,\texttt{idx}/P)$ features; Auto-Correlation also finds dominant lags on
its own. For this to be trustworthy, sampling must be uniform with no gaps (guaranteed here), the
period must stay stable over training and the horizon, and the peak must be genuinely significant
rather than an artefact of a red spectrum, which can be checked on separate sub-periods. Holidays
and other irregular events cannot be recovered this way.

\textbf{Q2.9 -- Structural models vs.\ decomposition Transformers.} Structural models (ETS, SARIMA,
state-space) win on short series (hundreds to a few thousand points), at low signal-to-noise and
with regular, stable seasonality, where their strong assumptions are roughly right and their few
parameters keep variance low. Transformers win on long series or many related series, with rich
covariates, non-linear interactions and several or changing seasonalities, where the data can pay
for the extra parameters. Before training I would compare the series length with the parameter
count (here 43k points against about 22k parameters, which is borderline), look at how regular the
spectrum is (a few sharp peaks favour structural models, a red, episodic spectrum favours flexible
ones), and fit a cheap structural baseline and a linear model with covariates first, accepting the
Transformer only if it beats them by more than the seed spread. In my case the Transformer did not
beat the training mean without covariates but did by a wide margin with future covariates, so its
advantage comes from using the covariates; a linear model with covariates would be the right next
baseline to test whether that needs a Transformer at all.
